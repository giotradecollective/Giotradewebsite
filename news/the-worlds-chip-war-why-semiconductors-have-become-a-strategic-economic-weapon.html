The World’s Chip War: Why Semiconductors Have Become a Strategic Economic Weapon

By Ethan R. Hayes, Markets & Investment Analyst  ·  Tech & Economy

Semiconductors power the modern world, from smartphones and vehicles to artificial intelligence. As the US and China compete for control of chip technology, these tiny components are becoming increasingly important to global economic and geopolitical power.

The world’s most powerful economies are competing for control over a technology most people rarely think about. Semiconductors power everything from smartphones and vehicles to artificial intelligence and military systems. As the United States and China compete for technological dominance, control over these tiny components is becoming a major source of economic and geopolitical power.

The Technology Behind the Modern Economy

Semiconductors, commonly known as computer chips, are essential to modern life. They process information, store data and allow electronic devices to perform complex tasks.

Their importance extends far beyond consumer electronics. Banks depend on computing infrastructure, vehicle manufacturers require chips for modern cars, and artificial intelligence systems rely on increasingly powerful processors.

This growing dependence has made semiconductor production a strategic priority for governments. A country that cannot secure access to critical chips may find itself vulnerable to supply disruptions, rising costs and technological dependence.

Why the United States and China Are Competing

The United States has a leading position in semiconductor design, advanced manufacturing equipment and technology development. China, meanwhile, has invested heavily in building its domestic chip industry and reducing its reliance on foreign suppliers.

The competition is not simply about producing better technology. It is also about determining who controls access to the infrastructure that will support future economic growth.

Export restrictions have become an important instrument in this rivalry. The United States has introduced controls on certain advanced semiconductor technologies destined for China, citing national security concerns.

This demonstrates how access to technology can increasingly be influenced by political relationships rather than commercial demand alone.

Taiwan: A Critical Link in the Global Supply Chain

One of the most important players in the semiconductor industry is Taiwan, home to Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC).

TSMC manufactures advanced chips for major technology companies around the world. Its specialised manufacturing capabilities make it a central part of the global technology supply chain.

However, this concentration creates a vulnerability. Any serious disruption to production in Taiwan could affect businesses across multiple industries and economies.

Other countries also play important roles. South Korea is a major producer of memory chips, Japan supplies specialised materials, and the Netherlands is home to ASML, whose advanced equipment is essential for manufacturing leading edge semiconductors.

The industry is therefore highly interconnected, making disruptions difficult to contain within one country.

Artificial Intelligence Is Increasing the Stakes

The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence has intensified demand for advanced processors, memory chips and data centre infrastructure.

Technology companies are investing heavily to build the computing capacity required to develop and operate AI systems. This has created opportunities across the semiconductor industry, from chip designers to equipment manufacturers.

However, the sector also carries risks. Manufacturing advanced chips requires substantial investment, specialised expertise and significant infrastructure. High demand does not automatically guarantee profitability, particularly when companies are spending heavily to expand production.

What This Means for South Africa and Investors

South Africa is not currently a major centre of advanced semiconductor manufacturing. Nevertheless, the global expansion of the industry creates opportunities in areas such as technical education, engineering, research, assembly and testing.

The country already has industrial and mineral resource capabilities that could support participation in selected parts of the wider technology supply chain. Realising this opportunity would require long term investment in skills, infrastructure and research.

For investors, the semiconductor industry offers exposure to several areas of technological growth. These include chip designers, manufacturing companies, memory producers and equipment suppliers.

However, investors should consider valuations, competition, capital requirements and geopolitical risks. A company operating in a rapidly growing industry is not automatically an attractive investment at any price.

GIOTRADE Network® Insight

The semiconductor race is about much more than computer chips. It reflects a wider shift in the global economy, where technological capability is becoming closely linked to national security, industrial competitiveness and economic influence.

As artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure continue to expand, countries that control critical parts of the semiconductor supply chain may gain significant strategic advantages.

For South Africa, the challenge is to move beyond being only a consumer of advanced technology and identify practical ways to participate in its development and production.

The next era of economic competition will not be determined by natural resources alone. Increasingly, it will also depend on who has the technology, expertise and industrial capacity to turn innovation into economic power.
