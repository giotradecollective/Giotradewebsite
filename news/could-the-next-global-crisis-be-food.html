Could the Next Global Crisis Be Food?

By Adrian K. Vale, Senior Markets & Investment Analyst  ·  Commodities

How El Niño, geopolitical conflict, energy disruption and rising agricultural risks are putting the global food system to the test.

Food security is becoming an increasingly important economic issue as changing climate conditions, geopolitical tensions and rising production costs place additional pressure on the global food system. The issue is not simply whether the world has enough food available, but whether food can remain affordable and accessible if several economic and geopolitical pressures develop at the same time.

Modern food production depends on a highly interconnected system involving agriculture, energy, fertilizer, transportation, trade and financial markets. A disruption in one part of that system can eventually create pressure somewhere else, meaning that an event that initially appears unrelated to food can ultimately affect agricultural production, commodity prices and household spending.

Weather disruptions can affect harvests, geopolitical tensions can influence energy markets, higher energy costs can increase the cost of fertilizer and transportation, while currency weakness can make imported commodities more expensive. Individually, many of these pressures can be managed, but the risk becomes significantly more important when several occur simultaneously.

The Global Food System Is More Than Agriculture

When consumers think about food production, the focus is usually placed on the farmer and the harvest. However, modern agriculture depends on a much larger economic network that stretches from the production of agricultural inputs all the way through to transportation, processing, distribution and retail.

Farmers require fertilizer, water, fuel, electricity, machinery and access to transportation, while food producers depend on processing facilities, storage, packaging and reliable distribution networks. International markets also play an important role because many countries depend on imported agricultural products, energy or farming inputs.

This means that the final price consumers pay for food is influenced by much more than the amount of food produced. Energy prices can affect farming costs, fertilizer prices can influence production decisions, fuel prices can increase transportation expenses and currency movements can change the cost of imported commodities.

The food system is therefore closely connected to the wider global economy. A disruption in one part of the system can eventually be transmitted through the rest of the supply chain.

El Niño and Agricultural Risk

Agriculture remains highly dependent on weather conditions, with rainfall, temperature and water availability all playing an important role in determining crop production and livestock conditions. One of the major climate patterns watched by agricultural markets is El Niño, which can influence weather patterns across different regions of the world.

The effects of El Niño are not necessarily the same everywhere. Some regions may experience drier conditions, while others can receive increased rainfall, creating different challenges for agricultural producers depending on their geographical location.

Changes in weather conditions can influence crop yields, planting decisions, harvesting conditions, water availability and agricultural incomes. If production is significantly affected, lower supply can potentially place upward pressure on agricultural commodity prices.

However, El Niño does not automatically result in a global food crisis. Modern agriculture has developed significant buffers through improved productivity, agricultural technology and food inventories. The greater concern arises when a weather disruption occurs at the same time as other economic pressures.

This distinction is important because the risk facing the global food system is not necessarily one single event, but the possibility that several different disruptions begin reinforcing one another.

The Connection Between Energy and Food

Energy is one of the less visible but highly important components of the global food system. Modern agriculture requires fuel to operate machinery, electricity for irrigation and processing, and energy throughout the transportation, storage and distribution process.

Energy is also closely connected to fertilizer production, which means that developments in global energy markets can have consequences for agricultural markets even when the original event has nothing directly to do with farming.

When geopolitical tensions disrupt energy markets, the effects can therefore extend beyond petrol and electricity prices. Higher energy costs can increase the cost of farming, fertilizer production, transportation, food processing, storage and distribution.

The process can eventually place pressure on food prices as higher production and transportation costs move through the supply chain.

For consumers, the original geopolitical event may be almost invisible. They may never directly experience the disruption in an oil market or shipping route, but they may eventually notice its effect through higher prices at the supermarket.

Why Fertilizer Matters

Fertilizer is another important part of the agricultural system and is essential for maintaining soil nutrients and supporting crop production. When fertilizer prices increase significantly, farmers are forced to make decisions that can influence both their current costs and future production.

Farmers may choose to absorb the higher cost, reduce fertilizer usage, change the crops they produce, reduce the amount of land planted or attempt to pass the additional cost through to buyers.

Each decision carries potential consequences. Reducing fertilizer use may lower costs in the short term but could potentially affect future yields, while passing higher costs through the supply chain may protect farmers from declining margins but increase costs for food processors and consumers.

If production is reduced significantly, the effect can extend further into commodity markets by placing additional pressure on future supply.

This makes fertilizer prices an important indicator to monitor because the food price problem can begin long before the final product reaches the supermarket.

Why Consumers May Feel the Impact Later

One of the most important characteristics of food markets is the time it takes for changes in commodity prices to reach consumers. Food does not move directly from a commodity market to a supermarket shelf. It passes through multiple stages, including agricultural production, harvesting, transportation, processing, packaging, storage, distribution and retail.

This creates a delay between changes in production costs and the final price paid by consumers. A farmer may experience higher fertilizer or fuel costs months before the consumer notices a change in supermarket prices. Food manufacturers may initially absorb some of the additional costs, while retailers may also delay passing those costs directly onto consumers.

As a result, a commodity shock can continue working its way through the economy even after the original event has begun to ease.

This is particularly important when analysing food inflation. A decline in commodity prices does not necessarily mean that supermarket prices will immediately decline because previous increases in production, transportation and processing costs may still be moving through the supply chain.

Food Availability Versus Food Affordability

There is an important distinction between having enough food available and being able to afford it. A country can have sufficient food supplies while households still experience significant financial pressure because of the price at which that food is available.

This becomes particularly important for lower income households, where food represents a larger proportion of total household expenditure. When food prices rise, households may have less income available for other goods and services.

Consumers may respond by purchasing cheaper alternatives, reducing the amount they purchase or cutting spending in other areas of their household budget. What initially appears to be a food price problem can therefore become a broader economic issue.

When households spend more of their income on basic necessities, there is potentially less money available for retail, entertainment, services and other forms of discretionary spending.

Food inflation can therefore influence not only household budgets but also wider consumer activity and economic growth.

Why Emerging Markets Can Be More Vulnerable

Global food shocks do not affect every country equally. Countries that rely heavily on imported food, fertilizer or energy can be particularly exposed to international price movements, especially when their domestic currencies are under pressure.

Many major commodities are traded internationally in US dollars. If global commodity prices rise while an emerging market currency weakens against the dollar, the domestic cost of those commodities can increase even further.

This can affect the cost of imported food, fuel, fertilizer, agricultural machinery and transportation.

For emerging market economies, the combination of higher international prices and currency depreciation can therefore create significant additional pressure. The global commodity price is only one part of the equation; the exchange rate can determine how severe that increase ultimately becomes for domestic consumers and businesses.

Why Southern Africa Matters

Southern Africa is particularly exposed to developments in agricultural markets because weather conditions, agricultural production and household food costs remain closely connected. Changes in rainfall can influence crop production, while higher fertilizer and fuel costs can increase the cost of farming and transportation.

These pressures can eventually affect households and businesses across the wider economy. Higher agricultural costs can influence food prices, while higher transportation costs can increase the cost of moving goods between producers, processors, distributors and retailers.

The consequences can therefore extend beyond the agricultural sector and influence household purchasing power, consumer spending, agricultural employment, food processing, transportation and retail activity.

For consumers, the final result may simply appear as a higher grocery bill. Behind that grocery bill, however, can be a combination of global commodity prices, weather conditions, energy markets, currency movements and transportation costs.

This is why developments in global agricultural markets matter to Southern African households even when those households are not directly involved in agriculture.

The Globalisation of Food

Modern food production has created a highly interconnected global system in which countries can specialise in the commodities they produce most efficiently, while agricultural products, fertilizer, energy and food products move across international borders.

This interconnectedness has created significant economic benefits. Producers can access larger markets, consumers can access a wider range of products and countries can specialise according to their comparative advantages.

However, interconnectedness also creates channels through which economic shocks can spread. A shipping disruption can affect commodity availability, a geopolitical conflict can affect fertilizer supplies, a drought can reduce agricultural exports, while an energy shock can increase farming and transportation costs.

The global food system is therefore both highly efficient and highly interconnected. This provides significant advantages during normal conditions, but it also means that disruptions in one region can have consequences far beyond their original location.

What Happens When the Shocks Occur Together?

The greatest risk may not come from one individual event. It may come from several manageable pressures occurring simultaneously.

Imagine a situation in which El Niño affects agricultural production while geopolitical tensions disrupt energy markets, oil prices increase, fertilizer becomes more expensive and shipping costs rise.

Agricultural producers could then face higher operating costs at the same time that transportation and processing become more expensive.

Food manufacturers may experience higher input costs, retailers may face margin pressure and consumers may eventually face higher prices.

At this point, what began as several separate events can become one broader economic problem.

A weather disruption can become an agricultural supply issue. An agricultural supply issue can become a commodity price issue. A commodity price issue can contribute to inflationary pressure, while higher food prices can reduce household purchasing power.

The original shock can therefore travel far beyond its initial source.

This is the central risk facing the global food system.

Technology Provides a Buffer

The outlook should not be viewed entirely through the lens of crisis. Agricultural technology has improved significantly and continues to provide farmers with new tools for managing production risks and improving efficiency.

Developments in precision agriculture, satellite monitoring, irrigation, drought tolerant crops, weather forecasting, automated machinery and digital farming can help producers manage resources more effectively and respond to changing conditions.

These developments provide an important buffer against climate and supply disruptions.

Agricultural innovation means that the global food system has significant capacity to adapt. The question is therefore not whether the system has resilience, but whether that resilience will be sufficient to absorb several simultaneous shocks.

What Should Markets Watch?

For investors, businesses and policymakers, several indicators can provide useful signals about the direction of global food markets.

Weather
Changing weather conditions in major agricultural producing regions remain one of the most important factors to monitor because sustained disruptions can influence crop yields and future supply.

Grain Inventories
Global food reserves can provide an important buffer against temporary production disruptions. Falling inventories can potentially increase the sensitivity of markets to future supply shocks.

Fertilizer
Fertilizer prices can provide an indication of pressure building within agricultural production. Significant increases may influence planting decisions and future yields.

Energy
Oil, diesel and electricity prices remain important because energy is required throughout farming, processing and transportation.

Shipping
Geopolitical developments affecting major trade routes can influence the cost and availability of internationally traded commodities.

Agricultural Commodities
Markets for wheat, maize, rice, sugar, coffee, cocoa and vegetable oils can provide important signals about global supply and demand conditions.

Currencies
Currency movements are particularly important for emerging markets because depreciation can increase the domestic cost of internationally traded commodities.

Food Inflation
Ultimately, one of the most important indicators is whether pressures in commodity and input markets begin to reach households.

These indicators should not be viewed individually. Together, they provide a broader picture of the condition of the global food system.

GIOTRADE Network® Insight

The next global crisis does not necessarily have to begin in a financial market. It could begin with weather, energy, fertilizer, geopolitical disruption or a combination of several pressures occurring simultaneously.

The important lesson is that economic risks rarely exist in isolation. The global food system connects climate, agriculture, energy, geopolitics, trade, currencies, transportation and household purchasing power.

A drought can affect agricultural supply. Energy prices can affect production costs. Fertilizer prices can influence future harvests. Geopolitical tensions can disrupt trade, while currency weakness can increase the cost of imported commodities.

Higher food prices can then reduce household purchasing power, creating consequences that extend beyond agriculture and into consumer spending and economic activity.

For now, the global food system has important buffers. Agricultural productivity continues to improve, technology is creating new ways to manage resources, global trade provides alternative sources of supply and food inventories can provide protection against temporary disruptions.

But resilience should not be confused with immunity.

The greatest risk may not come from one major event. It may come from several pressures occurring at the same time, with each pressure reinforcing the next.

Climate pressure can become agricultural pressure. Agricultural pressure can become commodity pressure. Commodity pressure can contribute to inflation. Inflation can reduce household purchasing power, and weaker purchasing power can eventually affect the wider economy.

The question for global markets is therefore not simply whether the world will have enough food. The more important question is whether the global food system can continue providing food at prices households can afford if the pressures surrounding it intensify.

That is the risk worth watching.
