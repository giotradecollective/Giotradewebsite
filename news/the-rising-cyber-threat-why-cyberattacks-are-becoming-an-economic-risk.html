The Rising Cyber Threat: Why Cyberattacks Are Becoming an Economic Risk

By Adrian K. Vale, Senior Markets & Investment Analyst  ·  Economy

Cyberattacks are increasingly targeting banks, businesses and governments. As digital dependence grows, protecting financial information and critical infrastructure is becoming an important economic priority.

Cyberattacks are becoming an increasingly serious threat to the global economy. Banks, businesses, governments and essential services face growing risks as criminals exploit weaknesses in digital systems. What was once largely considered a technology problem has developed into a broader economic concern, capable of disrupting businesses, exposing personal information and affecting services that millions of people depend on.

Why Are Cyberattacks Increasing?

As businesses and governments move more of their operations online, they create more opportunities for criminals to exploit digital systems.

Cyberattacks can be motivated by financial gain, political objectives, espionage or attempts to disrupt essential services. Some attackers seek to steal money, while others target confidential information or demand payments from organisations.

Artificial intelligence is also changing the threat landscape. Criminals can use it to create convincing fraudulent messages, imitate legitimate organisations and automate certain malicious activities.

However, many attacks still begin with relatively simple vulnerabilities, including stolen passwords, fraudulent emails, outdated software and unsecured computer systems.

Understanding the Different Types of Attacks

Not all cyberattacks work in the same way. Some target information, while others attempt to take control of systems or interrupt operations.

  • Ransomware: Criminals gain access to computer systems, encrypt important files and demand payment to restore access. Some also threaten to publish stolen information.
  • Phishing: Fraudulent emails, messages or websites trick people into revealing passwords, banking details or other sensitive information.
  • Data breaches: Unauthorised individuals gain access to confidential information, which may include identity numbers, financial records, customer details and business documents.
  • Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS): Attackers overwhelm websites or online services with excessive traffic, making them unavailable to legitimate users.
  • Cyberespionage: Attackers secretly access systems to collect confidential government, military, commercial or strategic information.

Although these methods differ, their consequences can extend far beyond the organisation initially targeted.

Why Banks Are Becoming Major Targets

Financial institutions are particularly attractive targets for cybercriminals because they manage valuable financial information, payment infrastructure and customer identities.

Recent incidents demonstrate how exposed the financial sector can be. In November 2026, French banking group Crédit Mutuel Arkéa disclosed a sophisticated cyberattack involving fraudulent transactions from one of its own accounts. The amount involved was reported to be approximately €100 million, although the final financial impact remained under assessment. The bank said that customer accounts were not affected.

In the United States, JPMorgan Chase and Mastercard also reported incidents involving third-party service providers, in which certain customer information, including names and email addresses, may have been accessed. These incidents did not involve any reported exposure of payment card data or disruption to their payment networks.

South Africa has experienced similar concerns. SA Home Loans disclosed unauthorised access to certain systems in November, potentially exposing customer names, bank account numbers, branch codes and some payment information. The company reported no evidence that home loan accounts had been accessed or customer records altered, and no financial losses had been identified at the time of its notice.

These examples highlight an important vulnerability: financial institutions do not operate in isolation. They depend on external software providers, payment networks and technology companies. A weakness in one of these connections can expose information or disrupt services, even when a bank’s main systems remain secure.

The consequences can include financial losses, identity theft, fraudulent transactions, operational disruptions and reduced public confidence in financial institutions.

The Wider Economic Cost

The financial consequences of cyberattacks extend beyond money directly stolen from victims. When a business is attacked, it may face operational shutdowns, lost revenue, recovery expenses, legal costs and reputational damage. Customers may lose confidence, while employees, suppliers and other businesses can also be affected.

The scale of the problem is becoming increasingly concerning. According to the South African government, a 2026 assessment estimated that cybercrime-related losses across Africa increased from US$192 million in 2024 to US$484 million in 2025.

In South Africa, the South African Banking Risk Information Centre (SABRIC) reported that digital banking crime losses reached R2.4 billion in 2025, compared with approximately R1.9 billion in 2024.

These figures demonstrate that cybercrime is no longer simply an issue for technology departments. It has become a material financial risk for businesses, institutions and households.

South Africa Is Not Immune

South Africa is increasingly exposed to the economic consequences of cybercrime.

The country’s financial sector, government institutions and private businesses all depend heavily on digital systems. As online banking, electronic payments and digital services become more widespread, the potential impact of cybercrime continues to grow.

For ordinary South Africans, the consequences can include stolen personal information, fraudulent transactions, identity theft and attempts by criminals to gain access to bank accounts.

A particularly concerning development is that stolen information can be used long after an initial breach. Criminals may combine personal details obtained from different sources to create convincing messages or telephone calls that appear to come from legitimate financial institutions.

Protecting these systems is therefore important not only for individual organisations but also for maintaining confidence in the wider economy.

What Does This Mean for Ordinary People?

Cybersecurity is not only the responsibility of governments and large corporations. Individuals are also exposed through their everyday digital activities.

A compromised email account can provide criminals with access to personal correspondence and information. Stolen identity details can be used in fraudulent applications, while compromised banking credentials may expose savings and financial accounts.

Even a seemingly legitimate message from a bank, delivery company or government department could be part of an attempt to obtain sensitive information.

Individuals should use strong, unique passwords, enable additional authentication where available, avoid suspicious links and never share banking PINs or one-time passwords.

It is also important to monitor bank statements and report unfamiliar transactions or debit orders to the relevant financial institution.

GIOTRADE Network® Insight

The growing cyber threat reflects a fundamental change in the global economy. As financial systems, businesses and public services become increasingly digital, the security of information and technology infrastructure is becoming just as important as the protection of physical assets.

For businesses, cybersecurity is no longer simply an IT expense. It is an essential part of operational continuity, financial risk management and customer protection.

For South Africa, strengthening digital security will be important in protecting financial institutions, public services and the confidence of people who increasingly depend on online platforms.

The modern economy depends on trust. If people cannot trust the systems through which they save, transact, communicate and conduct business, the consequences extend beyond technology.

In an increasingly connected world, protecting the digital economy is becoming an important part of protecting the economy itself.
