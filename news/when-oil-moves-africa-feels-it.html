When Oil Moves, Africa Feels It. How One Shipping Route Can Influence Fuel Prices, Food Costs and Inflation Across the Continent.

By Adrian K. Vale, Senior Markets & Investment Analyst  ·  Commodities

A barrel traded thousands of kilometres away can influence what millions of Africans pay at home.

The Strait of Hormuz is only about 33 kilometres wide at its narrowest point, yet it carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade. When tensions rise around this strategic waterway, the effects are rarely confined to the Middle East. Instead, they travel with every tanker, eventually reaching fuel stations, transport companies, supermarkets and households across Africa.

While global attention often focuses on the price of a barrel of oil, the greater story lies in how that price moves through an economy. For many African nations, the impact is not felt first in financial markets but in the everyday cost of living.

Not Every African Country Is Affected the Same Way

Although the Strait of Hormuz lies thousands of kilometres from Africa, disruptions along this vital shipping route can have consequences across the continent. Oil-exporting nations such as Nigeria, Angola, Algeria and Libya may benefit from higher crude prices through increased export revenues, while countries that depend heavily on imported fuel—including South Africa, Kenya, Ethiopia, Rwanda, Tanzania, Madagascar and Eritrea—are more likely to experience rising fuel costs, inflationary pressure and higher business expenses.

Why South Africa Feels the Pressure

South Africa is one of Africa's largest fuel-importing economies, making it particularly sensitive to movements in global oil prices. Because much of its crude oil and refined petroleum products are purchased from international markets, local fuel prices are influenced not only by the price of oil itself but also by the value of the rand against the US dollar. When either oil prices rise or the rand weakens, importing fuel becomes more expensive, increasing the likelihood of higher petrol and diesel prices for consumers.

The Chain Reaction

The effects of higher oil prices extend far beyond the fuel pump. More expensive fuel increases transport and logistics costs, making it costlier to move food, manufactured goods and essential supplies across the country. Businesses often face higher operating expenses, while households gradually experience rising living costs. As these increases spread across different sectors of the economy, they contribute to broader inflation, demonstrating why oil remains one of the world's most influential economic commodities.

More Than Just Fuel

Oil influences far more than private vehicles. Public transport systems, freight operators, airlines, agriculture, mining and manufacturing all depend on reliable and affordable energy. Farm machinery requires diesel, imported goods arrive through ports, and products travel hundreds of kilometres before reaching consumers. As a result, sustained increases in oil prices can influence almost every stage of the supply chain, affecting businesses and households alike.

Why Traders Watch Shipping Routes

Commodity traders monitor far more than oil production. The efficiency and security of global shipping routes are equally important because longer voyages, higher insurance costs or disruptions at strategic chokepoints can increase uncertainty across energy markets. This additional uncertainty is reflected in prices through what is commonly known as the geopolitical risk premium, where the possibility of future supply disruptions becomes part of today's oil price.

What GIOTRADE Network® Is Watching

The key question is whether current geopolitical tensions remain temporary or become a more persistent feature of global energy markets. If shipping conditions continue to improve and supply remains stable, some of today's risk premium could gradually fade. However, renewed disruptions or prolonged instability could keep oil prices elevated, placing continued pressure on fuel-importing economies across Africa. For consumers, that could mean higher transport costs, increased prices for everyday goods and a slower easing of inflation. For businesses and policymakers, it reinforces the importance of understanding how global events can shape local economic conditions.
