Should You Trust Financial Influencers? How Social Media Is Changing the Way Millions Learn About Investing

By Adrian K. Vale, Senior Markets & Investment Analyst  ·  Markets & Investing

Financial education has never been more accessible—but so has misinformation.

A decade ago, most people learned about investing through books, financial advisers, newspapers, or business television. Today, millions of investors begin their financial journey on X, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and countless online forums. Social media has transformed investing into something faster, more accessible, and more engaging than ever before.

This shift has undoubtedly expanded financial education. Complex topics such as exchange-traded funds (ETFs), inflation, compound interest, portfolio diversification, and risk management are now explained in minutes rather than textbooks. For many first-time investors, financial influencers—commonly known as finfluencers—have become the gateway into the world of investing.

But greater access to information does not always lead to better investment decisions.

The Rise of the Financial Influencer

The influence of social media personalities has grown alongside retail investing. Millions of people now consume daily market commentary, stock picks, trading strategies, and economic opinions through creators who often attract audiences larger than traditional financial media outlets.

Many financial influencers genuinely aim to educate. They simplify complicated financial concepts, encourage saving and investing, and help younger generations become financially aware much earlier than previous generations.

Their contribution to financial literacy should not be overlooked.

However, social media rewards attention, engagement, and speed—not necessarily accuracy.

When Popularity Is Mistaken for Expertise

One of the biggest risks facing modern investors is confusing popularity with credibility. Large follower counts, viral videos, and confident opinions can create an illusion of expertise. Yet many online personalities have no formal financial background, investment qualifications, or professional market experience. Others may selectively highlight successful trades while rarely discussing losses, creating unrealistic expectations of consistent profits.

Some creators also benefit financially from sponsorships, affiliate partnerships, referral commissions, or undisclosed commercial relationships. While these arrangements are not inherently problematic, they can create conflicts of interest that audiences may not immediately recognize.

Investors should remember that the algorithm rewards engagement—not investment performance.

Investing Is Personal

No investment strategy is universally suitable.

Every investor has different financial goals, income levels, investment horizons, tax considerations, and risk tolerance. A portfolio designed for a 22-year-old beginning their investment journey is unlikely to suit someone approaching retirement or managing institutional capital.

Short-form content rarely provides enough context to account for these differences.

Successful investing is built around personal financial planning—not copying someone else's portfolio.

Education Before Action

Social media can be an excellent place to discover ideas, learn new concepts, and stay informed about markets. It should, however, be viewed as the beginning of the research process rather than the end.

Before making any investment decision based on online content, investors should ask themselves:

  • Is this information supported by credible sources?
  • Is the creator explaining both the opportunities and the risks?
  • Are they educating their audience, or encouraging people to follow a trade?
  • Does this investment align with my own financial objectives and risk tolerance?

Good investing begins with asking better questions.

The Future of Financial Education

The democratization of financial knowledge is one of the most significant developments in modern investing. Never before have ordinary individuals had such immediate access to market commentary, economic data, educational resources, and investment discussions.

Yet the greatest skill in today's information-rich world is no longer finding information; it is evaluating it.

The investors most likely to succeed over the long term are not those who consume the most content, but those who learn to distinguish research from opinion, evidence from speculation, and education from entertainment.

What GIOTRADE Network® Is Watching

Financial influencers are reshaping how millions of people learn about investing, creating unprecedented access to financial education while also increasing the risk of misinformation. As social media continues to influence investment decisions, independent research, critical thinking, and disciplined risk management remain the foundations of successful investing. The most valuable voices in finance are often not those with the largest audiences, but those who encourage investors to think independently rather than simply follow the crowd.
