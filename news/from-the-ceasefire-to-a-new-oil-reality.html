From the Ceasefire to a New Oil Reality. Oil Pulls Back From the Highs — But the Risk Premium Remains

By Kago R. Modiega, Research Analyst  ·  Commodities

When Geopolitical Risk Returns, Oil Finds Its Premium.

The oil market has moved a long way in just a few weeks. After the June 18 US-Iran ceasefire reopened the Strait of Hormuz, Brent fell from an average of around $85 in June to below $70 by July 1 as tanker traffic resumed and expectations of improving supply returned.

Then the market turned again. Renewed conflict and disruption around major shipping routes pushed Brent above $100 on July 23, while WTI reached a July 23 peak of $92.19.

By July 31, prices had pulled back from those highs. Brent was around $90.04 and WTI around $84.87, although both benchmarks remained on track for gains of roughly 24% for July.

What Changed?

The biggest change is not simply the price of oil. It is the risk premium being attached to every barrel that has to move through an increasingly uncertain regional shipping network. Oil does not need to disappear for prices to rise. When traders believe that future supply could be disrupted, prices can move before a physical shortage appears. That is what makes geopolitical events so powerful in commodity markets.

Hormuz Remains at the Centre

The Strait of Hormuz remains one of the most important routes in the global energy system. The June reopening caused oil prices to fall sharply as shipping flows improved. EIA subsequently expected global crude production and trade flows to move closer to pre-conflict levels, putting downward pressure on prices.

But continued uncertainty around shipping has prevented the market from fully removing its risk premium. The situation has also expanded beyond Hormuz, with disruption around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait adding pressure to another important energy route.

What This Means for Consumers

The oil story eventually reaches far beyond trading screens. Higher crude prices can raise the cost of refined fuels, transportation and logistics. That can affect petrol, diesel, aviation and the cost of moving everyday goods. The effect is not one-for-one. Refining margins, taxes, exchange rates and local supply conditions all influence what consumers ultimately pay. But the longer energy prices remain elevated, the greater the pressure on households and businesses.

Why $100 Still Matters

Oil is currently below the July highs, but the $100 level remains an important reference. A sustained move back toward $100 would suggest that the market is once again assigning a greater probability to prolonged supply disruption. A continued move lower would tell a different story: shipping conditions are improving, production is recovering and part of the geopolitical premium is being removed.

GIOTRADE Network® Is Watching

The market appears caught between two possibilities. If shipping conditions continue to stabilize and supply recovers, crude could spend more time below the recent highs as the market returns its attention to inventories and demand. If disruption intensifies, oil could quickly revisit the $90s and potentially test the $100 area again. That makes volatility likely to remain an important feature of the market. For now, the more interesting question is not whether oil is simply bullish or bearish. It is whether the geopolitical risk premium is expanding or contracting.

The Consumer Outlook

The same uncertainty that drives crude prices can eventually influence fuel and transportation costs globally. A prolonged period of elevated oil prices could keep pressure on households and businesses, while a sustained improvement in supply routes could allow some of that pressure to ease. The direction of oil therefore matters far beyond the energy sector. It can influence inflation, transportation and the cost of everyday economic activity.
